Yes — I think there’s a very natural middle layer between curation and legitimacy/transparency.

I’d frame the three layers as:

1. Exam Curation — “Is this exam right for this student?”

This is your innermost circle.

The problem is misalignment between the student and what they're being asked to do:

Wrong/poor syllabus mapping
Overwhelming or irrelevant resources
Difficulty not matched to preparedness
Poor timing / cognitive load
One-size-fits-all assessment
Student's current state not accounted for
Expectations unclear or disconnected from preparation

Your software directly attacks this.

The key word I'd use is Curation because you're essentially arguing that an exam should be composed intelligently for its context, rather than being a static object.

2. Assessment Integrity — “Is the exam measuring what it claims to measure?”

I think this is your missing middle circle.

Once you've curated the right exam for the student, the next question is:

Can we trust that the result actually represents the student's knowledge, ability, or readiness?

This encompasses things like:

Validity — Does the exam actually measure the intended competency?
Reliability — Would the result be reasonably consistent?
Bias & accessibility — Is the result being distorted by factors unrelated to the intended skill?
Cheating / AI assistance — Is the demonstrated performance actually the student's?
Question quality — Are questions ambiguous, broken, misleading, or poorly calibrated?
Difficulty calibration — Is the assessment appropriately challenging?
Evaluation consistency — Would equivalent answers receive equivalent treatment?
Feedback loop — Does the exam reveal why someone succeeded or failed?

This is where your statement becomes really powerful:

Curation determines whether we're asking the right question. Integrity determines whether the answer means anything.

And this layer naturally requires software + institutional/policy adoption.

Your product could provide the infrastructure: question provenance, adaptive calibration, assessment analytics, AI-assisted item review, evidence trails, standardized evaluation, etc. But actually making assessment integrity universal requires schools, universities, examination boards, regulators, and policymakers to adopt those standards.

3. Legitimacy & Accountability — “Should we trust the system?”

This is your outermost circle.

Now we're no longer asking whether this particular exam was well designed.

We're asking:

Can society trust the institution and system that creates, administers, and acts upon these exams?

That's where you get:

Transparency
Accountability
Governance
Appeals
Auditability
Conflicts of interest
Policy fairness
Institutional incentives
Standard-setting
Who gets to define "merit"
How high-stakes decisions are made
Whether students have recourse when the system fails

And I agree with your instinct: you don't necessarily need to solve this layer directly with software.

Your product can provide the infrastructure and evidence that makes better governance possible, while acknowledging that the ultimate solution is institutional and political.

So the Venn becomes almost a hierarchy

I'd actually visualize it as three concentric circles, rather than a conventional Venn diagram:

OUTER — LEGITIMACY & ACCOUNTABILITY
Can we trust the system?

↓ depends on

MIDDLE — ASSESSMENT INTEGRITY
Can we trust the result?

↓ depends on

INNER — EXAM CURATION
Are we asking the right question?

And then your pitch becomes:

The future of exams isn't just about making exams smarter. It's about rebuilding trust from the inside out.

First: curate the right assessment for the right student.
Then: establish that the resulting assessment is valid, reliable, and meaningful.
Finally: use that foundation to enable transparent, accountable examination systems.

That's a very coherent product story because your scope of intervention gets deliberately narrower as you move outward:

Layer	Core question	Nature of solution	Your role
1. Curation	Is this the right exam for me?	Primarily software	We solve it
2. Integrity	Does my result actually mean what it says?	Software + standards/policy	We build the infrastructure
3. Legitimacy	Should I trust the institution/system?	Governance + policy + institutions	We enable & inform it
One subtle change I'd recommend

I wouldn't call circle 2 simply "Integrity."

For a hackathon pitch, "Assessment Integrity" is stronger because "integrity" can sound like anti-cheating. Your concept is much broader than that.

Other possibilities:

Assessment Integrity ← my favorite
Assessment Validity
Assessment Quality
Assessment Trust
Measurement Integrity
Evaluation Integrity

Of these, Assessment Integrity gives you the most room to talk about validity, bias, cheating, calibration, reliability, and consistency without making the layer feel too academic.

And there's a nice conceptual progression:

Curation → Integrity → Legitimacy

Right exam → Meaningful result → Trustworthy system.

That, IMO, is the cleanest backbone for your pitch.
